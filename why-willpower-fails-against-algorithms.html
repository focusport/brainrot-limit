Why Willpower Fails Against Algorithms (and What Works Instead)

When you lose an evening to scrolling, the usual reaction is self-blame: why can't I just stop? But that assumes it's a fair contest. It isn't.

By Brainrot6 min readUpdated Oct 11, 2026

On this page

  1. An unequal contest
  2. What works: pre-commitment
  3. What a good commitment device looks like
  4. Where Brainrot fits
  5. Take-aways

An unequal contest

When you open a feed, your attention meets a system that has been tuned, with enormous amounts of data and engineering effort, to keep people watching. It learns from every pause, replay and swipe. You are bringing one tired brain at the end of the day.

Self-control also varies. Research on decision fatigue is debated, but most people recognise that resisting temptation is harder when tired, stressed or hungry, which is exactly when feeds get used most.

Don't bring willpower to a fight against a recommendation system. Bring a rule you set in advance.

What works: pre-commitment

In Homer's Odyssey, Odysseus knew he couldn't resist the Sirens' song, so he had his crew tie him to the mast before they sailed past. He didn't try to be stronger in the moment. He changed the situation beforehand.

Behavioural scientists call this a commitment device, and it is one of the best-supported ways to follow through on intentions. Examples include automatic savings transfers, leaving the biscuits at the shop and setting a hard daily limit.

What a good commitment device looks like

  • Set in advance. You choose it when you're calm.
  • Automatic. It enforces itself without needing you to remember.
  • Targeted. It restricts the harmful thing, not everything, so you won't be tempted to remove it.
  • Hard to casually undo. A "five more minutes" button defeats the point.

Where Brainrot fits

Brainrot is built on this idea. You choose a daily video limit in the morning; in the evening the app enforces it for you, closing only the short-video feed and leaving messages, calls and long videos alone. You still make every decision. You just make it once, when it's easy.

Take-aways

  1. Struggling with feeds is normal. They are built to be hard to leave.
  2. Rely on structure, not mood.
  3. Make rules narrow enough that you'll keep them.
