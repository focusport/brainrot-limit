How to Stop Doomscrolling: 7 Tactics That Actually Work

You open your phone to check one message. Forty minutes later you're still swiping, tired and oddly unable to press the lock button. That isn't a lack of discipline. It's a predictable response to how feeds are built.

By Brainrot8 min readUpdated Oct 11, 2026

On this page

  1. Why doomscrolling is so hard to stop
  2. Why most fixes fail
  3. Seven tactics that help
  4. What to expect
  5. When to get extra support

Why doomscrolling is so hard to stop

Short-video feeds use what psychologists call a variable reward schedule. Most videos are forgettable, but every so often one is funny, outrageous or fascinating. Because you can't predict which swipe will pay off, each swipe carries a little anticipation. B.F. Skinner's experiments showed that unpredictable rewards produce some of the most persistent behaviour, and slot machines use the same principle.

Feeds also remove natural stopping points. There's no end of the page, no credits, no "next episode?" prompt. Autoplay decides for you, and the next video is already loading before you've finished thinking about the last one.

The feed doesn't need every video to be good. It only needs enough of them to keep your thumb moving.

Why most fixes fail

The usual first attempt is a timer on the whole app: 30 minutes on Instagram, 30 minutes on YouTube. It tends to collapse within days, for a simple reason: you need those apps. You need Instagram to answer a friend and YouTube for a tutorial. When the timer fires, you tap "ignore limit" for a legitimate reason, and the feed is right there.

The fixes that last do two things: they target the specific behaviour, and they take the decision away from the moment when you're tired.

Seven tactics that help

1. Limit videos, not apps

Cap the feed itself and leave the rest of the app alone. A tool like Brainrot counts each Reel or Short and closes the feed at your limit, so messages and long videos never get locked out and you never need to override anything.

2. Make the number visible

Scrolling distorts your sense of time. A small live counter (for example "27 of 40") breaks the trance in a way a clock doesn't, because it measures what you're actually doing.

3. Use one shared budget

If you only cap Instagram, the habit moves to YouTube Shorts. A single daily budget across all feeds closes that loophole.

4. Change the bedtime setup

Most doomscrolling happens in bed. Charge your phone across the room, ideally with a cheap alarm clock doing the waking-up job. If you can't reach it, you can't scroll.

5. Have a replacement ready

You reach for the feed when you're bored, anxious or tired. Put something low-effort in its place: a paperback by the bed, a podcast, a short walk. A gap with nothing in it gets filled by the phone.

6. Go greyscale during work hours

Colour is part of what makes feeds rewarding. Android's greyscale option (Digital Wellbeing → Bedtime mode, or Developer options on some phones) makes the screen noticeably less appealing.

7. Set the rule in the morning

Choose your limit when you're clear-headed and let software enforce it. A decision you made at 8am is much easier to keep than one you're making at 11pm.

A good starting pointCheck your current daily average for a couple of days, then set a limit about 25% below it. Lower it every week or two until it feels like a bit of a squeeze but not a punishment.

What to expect

The first few days feel boring, and that's the point. The urge to check usually peaks and fades within a couple of minutes if you let it. Most people find the urge weakens noticeably over two to three weeks of a consistent limit.

When to get extra support

If scrolling is seriously affecting sleep, work, relationships or mood, or you feel unable to cut back despite trying, speaking to a doctor or counsellor is a sensible step. Apps can help with habits, but they aren't a substitute for professional care.
